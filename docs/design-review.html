After Me, For You
Experience review · 30 October 2026

From a well-made form to the thing families thank you for

The foundations are strong. Right now, though, the experience is a careful, well-meaning form: around 50 topics and 200 fields, sorted into five filing cabinets. To be loved, it has to feel like talking to someone who knows exactly what to ask. It should take your life in the form it already comes in, which is your voice, photos and statements. And it should show your family, clearly, what they can now do.

Keep

What’s already right

  • “If you were in hospital tonight.” It’s the best idea in the product: urgent, true, and not morbid. It earns the right to talk about death later.
  • The 15-minute essentials and “Enough for now”. Permission to stop is rare in this category and very humane.
  • Finding bills from statements, on the device. This is the closest thing to magic in the prototype today.
  • The letter guidance and writing coach. It helps people start without writing for them.
  • The vault boundary. Passwords and card numbers never go into the plan your family reads.
Critique

What gets in the way

Most important first. Under each one: how it feels to the person using it, then the fix.

1

It feels like homework

“Something written in 4 of 8 topics. Only 46 to go.”

Fix: one conversation that always asks the next most valuable question. You never see the size of the job, only the next small step and what it achieved.

2

Too many doors

“Where do I even start?”

Fix: the welcome page has four ways in, the sidebar 13 destinations, and the overview six areas, two checklists and a “10 things” badge. Cut it to three places: Today, My plan, For my family.

3

It’s organised the way it’s built

“Is the spare key under Home, Admin or Personal?”

Fix: people think in situations (“if I’m in hospital”, “if I die”) and people (“what Jo needs”). Organise around those. The categories can stay underneath, as filters.

4

Typing is the bottleneck

“I’m not typing out all my tablets. They’re in the kitchen.”

Fix: the information already exists: pill packets, the Medicare card, insurance renewals, bills, the cover page of the will, the switchboard. Let people snap it, say it or upload it. Claude fills in the fields, and the person confirms them.

5

It asks before it gives

“Five pages of questions and I still don’t have anything.”

Fix: ask one or two things, then show straight away what that unlocked (“Jo can now get into the house”). The Life Scan’s questions get spread through the conversation, each asked when it matters.

6

Admin about the admin

“Do I set this to ‘In motion’ or ‘Come back when ready’?”

Fix: work out area status from what’s been written. For bills, ask the person to confirm what was found instead of setting up categories first. Configuration should be the exception.

7

Progress means nothing to a family

“Is 56% good? Good for what?”

Fix: show readiness as outcomes for named people: “If you’re in hospital tonight, Jo can get in, keep the bills paid and talk to your GP. She can’t yet deal with the bank for you.”

8

The family’s side is barely designed

“Mum’s in ED. Where’s the thing she made?”

Fix: right now the moment the product pays off is a document. Make it an app for the family: what to do today, who’s doing what, and the ability to ask the plan a question.

9

Voice is a footnote

“I just want to hear Dad again.”

Fix: the most treasured thing families keep is a voice. Make spoken answers and voice notes part of the main experience, not something promised “in the full app”.

10

It was built for a desk

“I’m at the bedside with my phone.”

Fix: capture happens in the kitchen, in the garage and at the bedside. Design for the phone first, with big tap targets and one hand. Then let it grow into a desktop layout.

Signature moments

Where “where has this been all my life” happens

Home screen

Today: one next step, and what it’s already bought you

The home screen stops being a dashboard of areas. It shows two things. The first is readiness, phrased as what named people can now do. The second is the single most valuable next step, sized to the time you have (“2 minutes”).

The bar at the bottom is always there: “Tell me anything…” with a mic and a camera. Say “the spare key’s under the frog by the back door” at any moment, and it gets filed in the right place.

Gaps are written as opportunities, never as failures. No badges, no streaks, no guilt.

Bills

Bills in a minute: one card per biller, three choices

Drop in your statements and get one sentence back: “27 things keep charging you, about $1,480 a month.” Then go through them one card at a time. Each card shows the evidence (the last few charges), with three big choices: Cancel it, Keep paying it, Hand it to Jo.

Categories, splitting PayPal and research all still happen, but out of sight. They sit under “Fine-tune” for people who want them. Today’s four-step flow becomes something you can finish in one sitting.

For the family

The family view: the first 48 hours, and the ability to ask the plan

When the plan is released (or the hospital summary is shared), the family gets an app, not a PDF. It shows what to do today, this week and later. Tasks can be given to people (“Sam: ring the bank”). And there’s one box: Ask Dad’s plan.

“Where’s the spare key?” brings back the answer in his words, with when he wrote it. This is the moment people tell their friends about, and it’s what sells the product to the next family.

Ask anything about the plan…

Your voice, kept

Record a 60-second hello next to each letter (“for Tom’s 18th”). Answer “What always made you laugh?” out loud. Families keep the recording and get a transcript as well.

Walk me round the house

A guided walk with the camera. At each stop, take a photo and say one sentence: the water main, the switchboard, the solar reset, the bins, the spare key. The result is an illustrated household manual.

Sunday afternoon mode

For doing it with a parent: two people, one phone. The adult child reads each question aloud and the parent answers in their own voice. Big type, easy to pause, consent built in.

Ask someone

Send one question to a family member: “Sam, where are the boat rego papers?” Their answer comes back for you to approve before it goes into the plan.

It keeps itself current

Upload statements again each quarter: “3 things changed. Kayo is new, AGL has stopped, Shannons went up 6%.” Life events (a new grandchild, a car sold) prompt a 2-minute update.

Hospital card on the lock screen

Medications, allergies and who to call, as a wallet card and a lock-screen image. One tap to show the ED nurse.

Design and layout

The whole frame, not just the flows

Today’s layout is a left sidebar with 13 destinations, area pages with clouds of topic chips, and long forms. The proposed frame has three places, one constant way to talk to it, and the plan taking shape alongside.

Three places, not thirteen

Today (talk, next step, readiness), My plan (everything written, grouped by situation) and For my family (preview what they’ll see, plus release). On a phone these are tabs along the bottom. Settings, the vault and Release sit under your name.

The bar is always there

“Tell me anything…”, with a mic and a camera, sits at the bottom of every screen. It removes the question “where does this go?” for good.

The plan takes shape beside you

On desktop, a right-hand panel shows each fact land in its place as you talk. That’s how a conversation still feels like progress. On a phone, it becomes a sheet you pull up.

One main action per screen

Today shows one next step, not ten suggestions. “Strengthen my plan” becomes the source of that next step instead of a badge counting up.

Cards are only for decisions

Confirmations and choices get cards. Everything else is calm text on the page, so the thing that needs you always stands out.

Daylight by default, dusk at night

The deep navy is beautiful, but in daylight it reads as late-night and heavy, especially to a 75-year-old. Make a warm light theme the default and keep the dusk palette as the dark theme. Keep the lamp as the one brand flourish, glowing a little brighter as readiness grows.

Bigger text, fewer words

18px body text, a one-tap text-size control (“Aa”), 44px tap targets, and plain labels. “In motion” and “Continuity Check” become “Started” and “How ready are you?”.

Pace the emotion

Start with the practical (hospital tonight) and earn the right to ask about death. Every heavy question has “Not today”, and the app honours it without nagging.

Feasibility

What the prototype can do now, and what needs the real app

Buildable now, in this prototype

  • The new frame: Today, My plan and For my family, the bottom bar and a daylight theme. All existing data carries over.
  • The conversation, with “Here’s what I heard” cards, using your Claude account to pull facts out of what you type or say.
  • Photos read by Claude: pill packets, cards, bills, renewal letters. This already works for bills. On a phone, the photo button opens the camera.
  • Speaking instead of typing, via the phone keyboard’s own microphone key.
  • Bills one card at a time, with the current detail kept under Fine-tune.
  • A preview of the family view, including Ask the plan.

Needs the production app

  • Recording voice and video inside the page (the prototype’s frame blocks the microphone and camera).
  • Keeping voice notes as keepsakes, and private speech-to-text.
  • Sending questions to family members, and their answers coming back.
  • Releasing the plan, with identity checks for whoever requests it.
  • Reminders, notifications and the lock-screen card.
  • Encrypted sync across devices and family members.
Recommendation

Build order

  1. The new frame and Today.Three places, the bottom bar, readiness written as outcomes, one next step and a daylight theme. It changes how everything feels without losing any data.
  2. Say it or snap it.The conversation with “Here’s what I heard” cards, starting with the 15-minute essentials. This is the flagship.
  3. The family view with Ask the plan.The payoff moment. It also tests how good the writing in the plan is.
  4. Bills in a minute.One card at a time, with the current four steps moved under Fine-tune.
  5. Walk me round the house, and Sunday afternoon mode.Then hand voice keepsakes and Ask someone to the production build.
Design for the risks

What could go wrong, and the guard for each

  • Claude mishears or misreads. Nothing is saved without a confirmation card, and the source photo or quote is shown beside what was pulled out.
  • Privacy. Show what will be sent to Claude before sending it. Never send the vault. Mask numbers to the last 4 digits before anything leaves the device, where possible.
  • Losing authorship. Claude asks and files. For letters and life story it never writes in the person’s voice. The words stay theirs.
  • Older users. Voice-first must never be voice-only. Everything also works by typing, at a large text size.
  • Emotional load. Pausing is easy, nothing counts down, and there are no streaks or guilt badges. Readiness only ever goes up.

The test for every screen: would a tired daughter at 2am, or a 78-year-old on a Sunday afternoon, know exactly what to do next?
